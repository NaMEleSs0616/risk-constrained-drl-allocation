\title{Do Tail-Risk Penalties Help Deep RL Portfolio Allocators? A Walk-Forward Evaluation with Tradable Execution, Market Impact and Pre-Specified Inference}
\author{Deborshi Vikramaditya Chakravorty}
\date{November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) portfolio allocators are often given tail-risk penalties in their reward to make them safer. We evaluate the most common form, per-step penalties on the agent\textquotesingle s rolling CVaR and drawdown, for proximal policy optimisation (PPO) on ten multi-asset ETFs over 14 annual walk-forward folds (2012--2026). Orders are decided after the close and filled at the next open, net of commission, spread and square-root impact, and every design is trained with five seeds. Earlier versions of this study filled orders at a close the policy had already observed; we corrected the simulator and tested five comparisons fixed in an analysis plan committed before the rerun. The test period had informed the earlier versions, so this is a reassessment under corrected execution, not an independent hold-out test.

In the pre-specified tests (two-level seed and time-block bootstrap, Holm correction), vanilla PPO trails equal weighting by 0.53 Sharpe (95\% CI {[}$-$0.79, $-$0.32{]}), the penalties have no detectable effect on the Sharpe ratio (+0.06 {[}$-$0.12, 0.26{]}), and slowing execution is the only design choice with a significant effect (+0.49 {[}0.27, 0.74{]}). The best agent is neither distinguishable from nor shown to be equivalent to 1/N ($-$0.08 {[}$-$0.23, 0.06{]}), and the pre-cost timing edge reported earlier, an artifact of the non-tradable fill, is withdrawn ($-$0.09 {[}$-$0.30, 0.12{]}). In secondary, uncorrected comparisons, the penalties lower daily CVaR and turnover, but by no more than holding the same cash in 1/N would; no agent raises its cash weight when volatility rises; and the untrained policy is as good as any trained agent. These results hold under other cost, impact and fill assumptions, with from-scratch training, and in four simulated markets. They concern this reward and state design, in which the CVaR penalty depends on return history the agent does not fully observe. Code, tests, analysis plan and outputs are public.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} deep reinforcement learning; proximal policy optimization; conditional value-at-risk; drawdown; market impact; execution timing; look-ahead bias; walk-forward validation; multiple testing; portfolio allocation

\section{Introduction}\label{s1}

Classical portfolio construction solves a static problem. Mean-variance optimisation \cite{r1} and its descendants choose weights from estimated moments and rebalance periodically. Estimation error in expected returns is large enough that the naive 1/N rule is hard to beat out of sample \cite{r4}, so practitioners lean on shrinkage \cite{r5} and on risk-based allocations such as equal risk contribution \cite{r6} and hierarchical risk parity \cite{r8}.

Deep reinforcement learning offers a state-dependent, non-linear allocation policy trained end to end on net performance \cite{r12,r13,r14,r16,r17,r53}. Because an agent that maximises expected return has no explicit reason to avoid tail losses, a common remedy is to put risk terms, such as a rolling CVaR or a drawdown, into the reward \cite{r47,r49,r50}. Whether such terms make an allocator better or safer \emph{out of sample, after realistic frictions, and beyond what simple portfolio rules achieve} is an empirical question, and the answer depends on evaluation details that are easy to get wrong: when orders are filled relative to the information used to make them, how training-seed variability enters the inference, and how many comparisons are made.

We study the most common member of this family, per-step penalties on the agent\textquotesingle s own rolling CVaR and drawdown,

\[ R_t = \log(1+r_t) - \lambda_1\,\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}_{\alpha,t} - \lambda_2\,D_t^2 , \]

in a simulator with commission, half-spread and square-root market impact \cite{r25,r26,r27}, and ask:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Research question.} Under tradable execution, realistic frictions and strictly out-of-sample evaluation, do tail-risk reward penalties make PPO allocation agents better or safer, relative to an unpenalised agent, to classical allocation rules and to the network\textquotesingle s own untrained starting point?
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Main finding.} They do not make the agents better than 1/N, and they make them only as much safer as holding cash would. How a PPO allocator trades governs its net performance far more than how its reward is shaped: slowing execution is the only design change with a significant effect. And the untrained network\textquotesingle s equal-weight-plus-cash prior is a benchmark no trained agent beats.
\end{quote}

\textbf{A correction.} Versions 1--5 of this study (circulated as \emph{Risk-Bounded Deep Reinforcement Learning for Dynamic Portfolio Allocation under Tail-Risk and Market Impact}) filled every order at the close of the same day whose close, range and volume the policy had just observed. That convention cannot be traded, and it inflated the fast-trading agents\textquotesingle{} results enough to create an apparent pre-cost timing edge over 1/N, which those versions reported as their most interesting positive finding. This version fixes the convention, reruns the decisive experiments with more seeds, and tests pre-specified hypotheses. The timing claim is withdrawn (\S{}5, \S{}6.4). The rerun pre-specifies the tests but not the research design: the same 2012--2026 test period had been examined in versions 1--5, so the corrected results are a methodological reassessment of those findings, not an independent confirmation (\S{}10). We also drop ``risk-bounded'' from the title: fixed reward penalties carry no guarantee of bounded risk, and we found no evidence of one.

\textbf{Scope.} The conclusions concern PPO with per-step penalties on realised history, one multiplier setting, a fixed trading speed, five seeds and one ETF universe whose test period is not new. Section 10 lists what they do not cover, including risk-sensitive RL objectives, states that expose the penalised risk measure, de-risking through the asset mix, and genuinely unseen data.

Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{An evaluation with tradable execution and pre-specified inference.} Decisions use data up to the close of day \emph{t}; orders are share quantities fixed at that close and filled at the open of \emph{t}+1. Five primary comparisons, the estimand (the expected out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of a newly trained agent), a two-level bootstrap over seeds and time, Holm correction, an equivalence margin and power calculations were committed before the rerun\textquotesingle s results were inspected (the test period itself had been used before; \S{}10).
\item
  \textbf{A documented execution-timing correction}: what the same-close convention does, how much it inflates the same trained policies, and regression tests that make it detectable (\S{}5).
\item
  \textbf{Controls that separate learning from portfolio construction}: an untrained-policy control, an exposure-matched (de-levered) 1/N, a decomposition of pre-cost performance into timing and static tilts, and regime-conditional de-risking diagnostics.
\item
  \textbf{Robustness checks}: costs from zero to three times the base case, no impact and tripled impact, an extra session of fill delay, an inherited starting book, warm-started versus from-scratch training, four independent simulated markets (one in which holding cash pays), and the frequency of reward clipping (\S{}7).
\item
  \textbf{An open, tested framework} (Gymnasium environment, cost model, baselines, inference) with every configuration and output.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{s2}

\subsection{Classical portfolio construction}\label{classical-portfolio-construction}

Markowitz \cite{r1} formalised the mean-variance trade-off; Black and Litterman \cite{r2} stabilised it with equilibrium returns; the Kelly criterion \cite{r3} maximises expected log growth, the return term of our reward. DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal \cite{r4} showed that the gains from optimisation are typically consumed by estimation error, making 1/N a demanding benchmark. Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage \cite{r5} regularises the covariance matrix; equal risk contribution \cite{r6,r7} and hierarchical risk parity \cite{r8} avoid expected returns. Coherent risk measures \cite{r11} and the linear-programming representation of CVaR \cite{r9,r10} made tail-risk optimisation tractable, and drawdown-constrained allocation has its own literature \cite{r36,r37}. Volatility-managed portfolios scale exposure inversely to recent variance \cite{r45}.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for allocation}\label{reinforcement-learning-for-allocation}

Moody et al. \cite{r12} and Moody and Saffell \cite{r13} trained trading systems by direct reinforcement on the differential Sharpe ratio; Almahdi and Yang \cite{r47} replaced it with a Calmar-ratio objective (return over expected maximum drawdown) for an ETF portfolio. Deep architectures followed \cite{r14,r15,r16}, including state augmentation with return forecasts \cite{r48}, market-condition embeddings that shift exposure using a drawdown-based risk measure \cite{r49}, and rewards that explicitly charge transaction and risk costs \cite{r50}. Libraries such as FinRL \cite{r17} standardised PPO-based \cite{r18} environments, and several studies compare DRL with mean-variance optimisation directly \cite{r51,r52}. Hambly, Xu and Yang \cite{r53} survey the area. Table~\ref{tab-litmatrix} places the closest studies against this one.

\subsection{Risk-sensitive and constrained reinforcement learning}\label{risk-sensitive-and-constrained-reinforcement-learning}

Constrained MDPs \cite{r24} add expected-cost constraints. Chow et al. \cite{r21} derived policy gradients for CVaR-constrained MDPs; Tamar et al. \cite{r20} estimated CVaR gradients by sampling; CPO \cite{r22} and RCPO \cite{r23} enforce constraints by trust regions and by adapting Lagrange multipliers on a slower timescale. Coache and Jaimungal \cite{r54} optimise time-consistent dynamic convex risk measures with an actor-critic method, and deep hedging \cite{r41} minimises convex risk measures of hedged P\&L under frictions. Our penalty sits at the simple end of this spectrum: fixed multipliers on a rolling empirical CVaR of realised returns rather than a distributional quantity of the return-to-go. That simplicity is what most applied studies use, which is why we test it.

\subsection{Transaction costs and market impact}\label{transaction-costs-and-market-impact}

Almgren and Chriss \cite{r25} separated temporary from permanent impact in optimal execution; empirical work supports a concave, roughly square-root dependence of impact on trade size relative to volume \cite{r26,r27}. Under quadratic costs, Gârleanu and Pedersen \cite{r46} show that the optimal policy trades a constant fraction of the way towards an aim portfolio, the motivation for our smoothed-execution variants.

\subsection{Evaluation methodology}\label{evaluation-methodology}

Backtest overfitting inflates Sharpe ratios when many configurations are tried \cite{r33}, and Arnott, Harvey and Markowitz \cite{r55} propose a research protocol, including prior hypotheses and full disclosure of trials, against it. Sharpe-ratio differences must account for serial dependence and cross-correlation \cite{r30}; we use circular block resampling \cite{r29}. In RL, results vary strongly across seeds, so interval estimates over seeds are needed \cite{r31,r32}. We combine both levels in one bootstrap, adjust the primary family with Holm\textquotesingle s procedure \cite{r56}, and test equivalence with two one-sided tests \cite{r57}.

\section{Problem formulation}\label{s3}

\subsection{Markov decision process}\label{markov-decision-process}

Time is indexed by trading days \(t\). The investor holds \(N\) risky assets and cash, with net asset value \(\mathrm{NAV}_t\) marked at the close, share holdings \(h_t\in\mathbb R^N\), close prices \(P_t\) and open prices \(O_t\). Decisions are taken after the close of \(t\) using information available at that close.

\textbf{State.} The observation is

\[ s_t = \big(\,X_{t-L+1:t},\; w^-_t,\; w^{\text{cash}}_t,\; D_t,\; \hat\sigma^{p}_t\,\big), \qquad X\in\mathbb R^{L\times N\times F}, \]

where \(X\) holds \(F=20\) causally normalised features per asset over \(L=20\) days (\S{}4.3), \(w^-_t\) are the current (drifted) weights, \(D_t = 1-\mathrm{NAV}_t/\max_{\tau\le t}\mathrm{NAV}_\tau\) is the running drawdown, and \(\hat\sigma^p_t\) is the 21-day realised portfolio volatility. Entries are clipped to \([-10,10]\); the observation has \(LNF + N + 3\) dimensions (4,013 for ten assets).

\textbf{Action.} The policy outputs \(a_t\in[-1,1]^{N+1}\), mapped to target weights by a temperature-scaled softmax with an explicit cash logit,

\[ (w^\star_t, w^{\text{cash}\star}_t) = \operatorname{softmax}(\kappa\, a_t),\qquad \kappa = 3, \]

so the agent is long-only and fully funded and can de-risk continuously into cash. At initialisation the actor\textquotesingle s mean action is close to zero, so the untrained policy holds about \(1/(N+1)\) in every asset and in cash.

\subsection{Execution timing and friction-adjusted returns}\label{execution-timing-and-friction-adjusted-returns}

\textbf{Timeline.} At the close of \(t\) the agent observes \(s_t\) and chooses target weights. The order is a vector of \emph{share quantities} fixed at that moment from information up to the close of \(t\). It is filled at the open of \(t+1\); the existing book is carried overnight, so it earns (or loses) the overnight gap \(O_{t+1}/P_t\). Costs are charged on the executed notional at the open, using volatility and average daily volume (ADV) known at \(t\).

\textbf{Order sizing.} With decision-time NAV and weights \(w^-_t = h_tP_t/\mathrm{NAV}_t\), the order is

\[ n_t = \frac{\operatorname{clip}\!\big(\tilde w_t\,\widetilde{\mathrm{NAV}}_t - h_t P_t,\; -\rho\,\mathrm{ADV}_t,\; \rho\,\mathrm{ADV}_t\big)}{P_t}, \]

where \(\widetilde{\mathrm{NAV}}_t\) is the NAV net of the cost of the trade itself, found by a short fixed-point iteration at decision-time prices, \(\rho = 10\%\) caps participation, and \(\tilde w_t = w^\star_t\) except under smoothed execution (below). The executed notional is \(q_t = n_t \odot O_{t+1}\). Because the order is fixed in shares, an overnight gap moves the executed notional slightly away from the target; for 1/N this leaves at most 0.5\% of NAV as negative cash on typical days (2.5\% in the worst case), financed at the zero cash rate.

\textbf{Costs.} With \(\sigma_{i,t}\) the 20-day daily volatility and \(\mathrm{ADV}_{i,t}\) the 20-day mean dollar volume (both up to \(t\)), the per-unit square-root impact is \(I_{i,t} = \sigma_{i,t}(|q_{i,t}|/\mathrm{ADV}_{i,t})^{1/2}\), and the dollar cost is

\[ C_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\underbrace{(c+s)\,|q_{i,t}|}_{\text{commission + half-spread}} + \underbrace{\eta\, I_{i,t}\,|q_{i,t}|}_{\text{temporary}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\gamma\, I_{i,t}\,|q_{i,t}|}_{\text{permanent, own trade}} + \underbrace{\psi_{i,t}\, q_{i,t}}_{\text{residual displacement}}\Big], \]

where permanent impact is a per-asset displacement state that each trade pushes in its own direction and that decays with half-life \(h\): \(\psi_{i,t+1} = \delta(\psi_{i,t} + \gamma I_{i,t}\operatorname{sign}(q_{i,t}))\), \(\delta = 2^{-1/h}\). Positions are marked at historical, un-impacted prices, so impact never creates paper gains; a round trip on a flat price path always loses money (tested).

\textbf{Accounting.}

\[ \text{cash}^{+}_t = \text{cash}_t - n_t^\top O_{t+1} - C_t,\qquad h^{+}_t = h_t + n_t,\qquad \mathrm{NAV}_{t+1} = \text{cash}^{+}_t + h^{+\top}_t P_{t+1},\qquad r_t = \frac{\mathrm{NAV}_{t+1}}{\mathrm{NAV}_t} - 1 . \]

Gross-of-cost returns add \(C_t/\mathrm{NAV}_t\) back to \(r_t\). Parameters: \(c = 1\) bp, \(s = 2\) bp, \(\eta = 0.15\), \(\gamma = 0.05\), \(h = 5\) days, \(\rho = 10\%\), initial NAV \$10m per fold. On days when a rule does not rebalance it sends no order at all, so its book simply drifts at zero cost.

\textbf{Smoothed execution (variants S and SE).} The weights actually targeted are a partial adjustment from the current weights towards the policy\textquotesingle s target, with a no-trade band \(b\):

\[ \tilde w_t = w^-_t + \kappa_{\mathrm{tr}}\,(w^\star_t - w^-_t),\qquad \tilde w_t \leftarrow w^-_t \ \text{ if } \ \lVert \tilde w_t - w^-_t\rVert_1 < b , \]

with \(\kappa_{\mathrm{tr}} = 0.1\) (a half-life of about a week) and \(b = 1\%\). Both are round values chosen when smoothed execution was introduced in an earlier version, and were not tuned; a faster or slower speed could do better or worse. The policy supplies the aim portfolio and the environment applies a fixed trading speed \cite{r46}.

\subsection{Tail-risk measures}\label{tail-risk-measures}

For losses \(\ell = -r\), CVaR at level \(\alpha\) is the expected loss in the worst \(1-\alpha\) tail \cite{r43}. We use the Rockafellar--Uryasev representation \cite{r9,r10}, exact for empirical distributions:

\[ \widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}_{\alpha,t} = \widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_{\alpha,t} + \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)W}\sum_{k=0}^{W-1}\big(\ell_{t-k} - \widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_{\alpha,t}\big)^+ , \]

with \(\alpha = 0.95\), a rolling window of \(W = 63\) realised returns and at least 20 observations before the penalty switches on.

\subsection{Risk-penalised reward}\label{risk-penalised-reward}

\[ R_t = k\cdot\operatorname{clip}_{[-10/k,\,10/k]}\Big( \log(1+r_t) \;-\; \lambda_1\big[\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}_{\alpha,t}\big]^+ \;-\; \lambda_2\, D_{t+1}^2 \Big),\qquad k = 100, \]

and PPO maximises \(J(\pi) = \mathbb E_\pi\big[\sum_t \gamma^t R_t\big]\). \(J\) is the Lagrangian of a constrained MDP evaluated at fixed multipliers: nothing sets a target risk level, so the penalties move the optimum along the return/risk frontier by an amount that depends on their scale, and no bound on risk follows. The CVaR term depends on 63 days of the agent\textquotesingle s own returns, which the state summarises only through \(D_t\) and \(\hat\sigma^p_t\), so the reward is not Markov in \(s_t\): the agent cannot see its current CVaR estimate, or which past losses are about to enter or leave the window, and has to learn its response to tail risk from these proxies. The problem is therefore partially observed, which may limit how well a penalised agent can learn to manage tail risk. Our findings about the penalties apply to this reward-and-state design; a state containing the rolling CVaR and VaR, the return window itself or a recurrent summary of it would remove the mismatch, and we have not tested one (\S{}10). In daily data the return term is small (about \(3\times10^{-4}\) for 20\% volatility, against a daily CVaR of about \(2.5\times10^{-2}\)), so we use multipliers scaled to daily magnitudes, \((\lambda_1,\lambda_2) = (0.05, 0.5)\) (variant E). At these values a typical CVaR penalty (\(0.05\times2.5\times10^{-2}\approx1.3\times10^{-3}\)) and the drawdown penalty at a 7\% drawdown (\(0.5\times0.07^2\approx2.5\times10^{-3}\)) are several times the mean daily log return but an order of magnitude below its daily standard deviation (about \(1.3\times10^{-2}\)): large enough to move the optimum, small enough not to swamp the return signal. This is one point on the trade-off, set by this scale argument and not tuned on results; other multipliers could behave differently. The legacy runs also used \(\lambda\in\{0,1\}\), where the penalties dominate the return term (\S{}8). The clip rarely binds (\S{}7.4).

\subsection{Policy architecture and optimisation}\label{policy-architecture-and-optimisation}

Each asset\textquotesingle s \(L\times F\) history passes through a shared causal temporal convolutional encoder \cite{r38} (two dilated layers, kernel 3, width 32, receptive field 7 days); a learned asset embedding is added, one pre-norm transformer block \cite{r39} (4 heads) mixes information across assets, and the result is fused with an embedding of the portfolio state into a 128-dimensional vector shared by a Gaussian actor (state-independent log-std, initialised at $-$0.5) and a critic, each with hidden layers {[}128, 64{]} and tanh activations. Agents are trained with PPO \cite{r18} and generalised advantage estimation \cite{r19} in Stable-Baselines3 \cite{r44} with the hyper-parameters in Table~\ref{tab-hparams}. Rewards are normalised by a running estimate of the return scale; observations are not, because the features are already z-scores. Training episodes are 252 days long, start at random dates inside the training window, begin in cash, and use the same next-open execution as evaluation.

\section{Empirical setup}\label{s4}

\subsection{Market data}\label{market-data}

The study uses ten liquid U.S.-listed ETFs covering the major asset classes: U.S., developed ex-U.S. and emerging equities (SPY, EFA, EEM); intermediate and long Treasuries (IEF, TLT); investment-grade and high-yield credit (LQD, HYG); gold (GLD); broad commodities (DBC); and U.S. real estate (VNQ). Daily open, high, low, close and volume come from Yahoo Finance, adjusted for splits and distributions. The sample runs from 11 April 2007 (HYG\textquotesingle s first trading day) to 14 October 2026: 4,910 trading days with no gaps or zero-volume days. The raw file is not redistributed; \texttt{scripts/download\_data.py} rebuilds it and records its provenance, and the release manifest records its SHA-256 hash (\S{}12).

\subsection{Simulated markets}\label{simulated-markets}

Simulated markets provide what real data cannot: ground-truth regimes and independent replications. A two-state Markov regime in the spirit of Hamilton \cite{r40} drives five assets: calm turns to stress with probability 0.008 per day and stress persists with probability 0.97. Assets follow a one-factor model with Student-t innovations ($\nu$ = 4); in stress, volatility rises 2.5-fold, factor loadings rise from 0.55 to 0.85 and drift falls to $-$35\% a year. Calm-regime drifts are drawn between 2\% and 10\% a year, and each asset carries a weak, persistent latent alpha (AR(1) coefficient 0.97, scale 0.06 daily standard deviations). Opens are the previous close times a random overnight gap. Each market has 3,000 days. For the robustness study (\S{}7.3) we draw three new standard markets and one \textbf{bear market} with calm-regime drifts between $-$8\% and 0\% a year and stress twice as frequent, a setting in which holding cash pays and a tail-risk-aware agent should beat 1/N. The legacy controlled study (\S{}8) used a single market (appendix Table~\ref{tab-syn-data}).

\subsection{Features}\label{features}

For each asset and window \(w\in\{5,21,63\}\) we compute log momentum, realised volatility, distance from the moving average, log relative volume, and two order-flow-imbalance proxies: bulk-volume classification \cite{r28} and close-location-value-weighted volume. The one-day return and the log high/low range complete the set (\(F = 20\)). Every feature is z-scored with a rolling 252-day window using data up to and including \(t\) and clipped to $\pm$5. Features use the close, range and volume of day \(t\), all known at the decision time. Unit tests confirm that perturbing any data after \(t\) leaves every feature, the observation, the target weights and the order at \(t\) unchanged, and that features are invariant to rescaling the price history (so later dividend or split adjustments cannot leak in).

\subsection{Walk-forward protocol}\label{walk-forward-protocol}

We use 14 rolling, end-anchored folds, each with a 1,008-day (four-year) training window, a 5-day embargo and a 252-day test window. The test windows form one contiguous track of 3,527 daily returns from 2 October 2012 to 14 October 2026 (fold calendar in Table~\ref{tab-fold-cal}). Each (variant, seed) is trained as a chain: fold 0 trains from scratch for 102,400 steps, and each later fold fine-tunes the previous fold\textquotesingle s model on its own training window for 28,672 steps. Only data before each test window is used for training. Each fold is evaluated with its final model, with no checkpoint selection. \S{}7.2 compares warm-started with from-scratch training. The simulated markets use the same chain protocol with four folds.

\subsection{Strategies}\label{strategies}

\textbf{PPO variants} (5 seeds each) share every hyper-parameter (Table~\ref{tab-hparams}):

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A}: return only ($\lambda_1$ = $\lambda_2$ = 0);
\item
  \textbf{E}: calibrated CVaR and drawdown penalties ($\lambda_1$ = 0.05, $\lambda_2$ = 0.5);
\item
  \textbf{S}: return only, with smoothed execution (\(\kappa_{\mathrm{tr}} = 0.1\), \(b = 1\%\));
\item
  \textbf{SE}: smoothed execution with E\textquotesingle s penalties.
\end{itemize}

These four were chosen, before the rerun, as the variants that decide the paper\textquotesingle s claims. Variants that appear only in the legacy runs (CVaR-only and drawdown-only penalties at $\lambda$ = 1, a turnover penalty, RCPO and a volatility overlay) are summarised in \S{}8.

\textbf{Classical baselines}, long-only and fully invested, on trailing 252-day estimates and executed through the same simulator: 1/N rebalanced monthly (every 21 days) and daily, and 1/N buy-and-hold \cite{r4}; mean-variance \cite{r1} with Ledoit--Wolf covariance \cite{r5}, risk aversion 5 and sample means shrunk halfway to their cross-sectional average; equal risk contribution \cite{r6,r7}, monthly and daily; and hierarchical risk parity \cite{r8}.

\textbf{Controls.} The \emph{untrained policy} holds \(1/(N+1)\) in every asset and in cash, rebalanced daily: PPO\textquotesingle s starting point, which shows what training adds. The \emph{exposure-matched control} scales 1/N by each agent\textquotesingle s average invested fraction, holding the rest in cash, which shows what an agent\textquotesingle s cash buffer alone is worth.

\subsection{Metrics and pre-specified inference}\label{metrics-and-pre-specified-inference}

We report cumulative and annualised return, volatility, the Sharpe ratio (zero risk-free rate), the Sortino ratio \cite{r34}, maximum drawdown, the Calmar ratio \cite{r35}, daily CVaR\textsubscript{95}, turnover and costs, computed on each seed\textquotesingle s stitched test track. The analysis plan (\texttt{ANALYSIS\_PLAN.md}) was committed before any rerun result was inspected and fixes:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Estimand.} For a PPO variant, the expected out-of-sample metric of a newly trained agent, estimated as the mean of per-seed metrics. Baselines are deterministic.
\item
  \textbf{Two-level bootstrap.} Each replication resamples each variant\textquotesingle s seeds with replacement, then resamples days in circular 21-day blocks with the same indices for every series, and recomputes the difference (2,000 replications; percentile intervals; two-sided p-values from the bootstrap distribution centred under the null).
\item
  \textbf{Primary family} (net Sharpe unless stated; Holm-adjusted at $\alpha$ = 0.05): P1 E $-$ A (do penalties change performance?); P2 S $-$ A (does smoothing?); P3 SE $-$ 1/N (does the best-designed agent differ from 1/N?); P4 A $-$ 1/N; P5 gross A $-$ gross 1/N (is there a pre-cost timing edge?).
\item
  \textbf{Equivalence.} Two strategies are equivalent only if the 90\% interval lies inside $\pm$0.15 Sharpe (TOST at $\alpha$ = 0.05); a non-significant difference that fails this test is reported as inconclusive, never as parity.
\item
  \textbf{Power.} The minimum detectable effect at 80\% power, MDE = 2.80 $\times$ bootstrap SE.
\item
  \textbf{Decision rule.} If P5 is not significant after correction, the timing claim of versions 1--5 is withdrawn.
\end{itemize}

All other comparisons, including every comparison of CVaR, drawdown and turnover, are secondary: they were not pre-specified as a family, are not corrected for multiplicity, and are exploratory.

\section{The execution-timing correction}\label{s5}

\subsection{What was wrong}\label{what-was-wrong}

Versions 1--5 of this study computed features from the close, range and volume of day \(t\) and then filled the resulting order at the close of the same day \(t\). A real investor cannot see the closing price and also trade at it: the decision would have to be made before the information it uses exists. The error matters most for agents that trade a lot on short-horizon features. A policy that reacts to the day\textquotesingle s last move at the price where that move ended can capture short-term reversals from the close onwards, part of which, in practice, happens overnight before any order can be filled. Slow strategies are hardly affected: the monthly baselines changed by at most 0.012 Sharpe between conventions.

\subsection{How it was fixed and verified}\label{how-it-was-fixed-and-verified}

The simulator now separates the decision from the fill (\S{}3.2): orders are share quantities fixed at the decision close and filled at the next open, the old book is carried overnight, and cost inputs are known at the decision. A first rerun was stopped after about 30 minutes, before any result was inspected, when a further audit found that the order had been converted from weights into shares at the open of \(t+1\); the analysis plan was amended accordingly before restarting (Amendment 1). The test suite now checks, among other things:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  that the observation, the target weights \emph{and the order} at \(t\) are unchanged when every price, volume and open from \(t+1\) onwards is perturbed;
\item
  a two-asset, three-day example whose NAV path, cash, holdings and costs are computed by hand;
\item
  that existing positions earn the overnight gap and costs are charged on executed quantities at the fill price;
\item
  that cash and weights stay consistent (weights plus cash sum to one; long-only books never go short) under large random gaps;
\item
  that gross return equals net return plus cost, and that a buy-and-hold book never re-trades.
\end{itemize}

The look-ahead checklist in the analysis plan lists every information pathway (features, order generation, fill, cost inputs, fold boundaries, reward normalisation, baselines, price adjustments) and how each is controlled.

\subsection{The untrained policy}\label{the-untrained-policy}

The untrained policy matches 1/N (Sharpe 0.64), has the smallest maximum drawdown of all strategies (18.4\%) and is at least as good as every trained agent: SE $-$ untrained is $-$0.08 ({[}$-$0.23, 0.06{]}) and A $-$ untrained $-$0.53 ({[}$-$0.79, $-$0.31{]}). Training moves the policy away from a diversified, partly de-levered prior mainly by adding turnover; smoothing and penalties help mostly by limiting how far it moves. The untrained policy also matches 1/N to within 0.01 Sharpe in all four independent simulated markets of \S{}7.3, where PPO-S is never significantly different from 1/N.

The base scenario reproduces the main results exactly, so differences in Table~\ref{tab-sens-no} come only from the perturbation. Three findings stand out.

\textbf{R1.} \textbf{No agent beats 1/N even without costs.} With every friction removed, the agents\textquotesingle{} Sharpe ratios are 0.56--0.64, against 0.65 for 1/N and the untrained policy: the same conclusion as the pre-specified P5, now for all four variants and without relying on the gross-up of costs.

\textbf{R2.} \textbf{Smoothing helps only by saving costs.} The benefit of smoothing (P2) grows with costs, from +0.08 ({[}$-$0.14, 0.29{]}) with no costs to +0.29 at half costs, +0.49 in the base case and +1.30 at triple costs. Removing impact entirely (fees and spread only) or tripling it changes the smoothed agents\textquotesingle{} Sharpe ratios by less than 0.01 and the unsmoothed agents\textquotesingle{} by up to 0.15. Filling one session later, at the next close, changes no conclusion. Starting every fold from an inherited 1/N book instead of cash raises the smoothed agents\textquotesingle{} Sharpe ratios by about 0.02, about as much as it raises 1/N\textquotesingle s own (+0.01).

\section{What remains of the earlier results}\label{s8}

Versions 1--5 also ran variants that were not rerun: CVaR-only and drawdown-only penalties at $\lambda$ = 1 (B, C, D), a turnover penalty (T), RCPO with loose and with 1/N-matched targets (R, RT, SRT), a volatility-targeting overlay, and a controlled 2$\times$2 ablation on a simulated market with validation-based checkpoint selection. All of these used the same-close convention. Their absolute numbers are therefore not tradable and are not used for any claim here. Comparisons among slowly trading strategies, which the convention barely affects (\S{}5.3), and qualitative patterns that the corrected rerun reproduces remain informative. Three such patterns are worth recording:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Training past a short budget made agents worse out of sample.} On the legacy simulated market, vanilla PPO\textquotesingle s Sharpe ratio fell from 1.24 at 50k steps to 1.04 at 150k while its in-sample Sharpe kept rising, and the untrained policy beat every trained agent at every budget (appendix Table~\ref{tab-checkpoints-syn}). The corrected ETF study again finds the untrained policy unbeaten (\S{}6.6).
\item
  \textbf{Validation-based checkpoint selection interacted with the penalties}, favouring early, less-trained checkpoints for penalised variants. This is why the ETF study uses no selection.
\item
  \textbf{Binding RCPO constraints did not make the agents raise cash as risk rose} (appendix Table~\ref{tab-lagrangian-fu}), and neither did any fixed penalty, the same conclusion as \S{}6.5.
\end{itemize}

The legacy paper, its tables and its outputs are preserved in the repository (\texttt{paper/legacy/}, \texttt{outputs\_real/}, \texttt{outputs\_real\_followup/}, \texttt{outputs/}) with their configurations pinned to the same-close convention, so every earlier number remains reproducible.

\section{Discussion}\label{s9}

\subsection{Fill conventions matter most for the agents that look best}\label{fill-conventions-matter-most-for-the-agents-that-look-best}

The same-close convention changed the verdict on exactly the strategies that trade most. It turned a gross deficit into a gross advantage for every unsmoothed agent and produced a timing story that a static-tilt decomposition and a paired bootstrap appeared to confirm. Neither check could catch it, because both take the simulated fills as given. Two practices would have caught it earlier: specifying the fill price relative to the information set in the environment\textquotesingle s interface (here, separate decision and execution prices, with a test that perturbs everything after the decision), and routinely evaluating the same trained policies under a delayed fill (\S{}7.1). We suspect the problem is common in applied DRL allocation, where daily close-to-close environments are the default.

\subsection{Execution design versus reward shaping}\label{execution-design-versus-reward-shaping}

Under tradable execution, the agents\textquotesingle{} problem is not that they find a signal and trade it away (the earlier story) but that they find no tradable signal and still trade heavily. Exploration noise in training keeps turnover high, the policy re-targets every day, and every change of view is paid for. Slowing execution is the only intervention that moved the Sharpe ratio significantly, and it did so by limiting damage rather than by enabling skill. Tail-risk penalties act on a different margin: they lower turnover a little and hold more cash, which lowers CVaR and drawdown about as much as the same cash in 1/N does.

\subsection{Penalties as regularisers towards the prior}\label{penalties-as-regularisers-towards-the-prior}

PPO\textquotesingle s softmax policy starts as a diversified, partly de-levered portfolio, and that portfolio is hard to beat. Return-only training pulls the policy towards concentrated, high-turnover allocations that fit the training window; drawdown penalties and smoothing make those excursions costly or slow, so the policy stays nearer its prior. That reading explains why the penalties reduce risk without improving the Sharpe ratio, why no agent raises cash as volatility rises, and why the untrained policy is the strongest ``agent'' in both the corrected and the legacy studies.

\subsection{What a better design would need}\label{what-a-better-design-would-need}

An allocator that adds value over 1/N under tradable execution would need a signal that survives the overnight gap and costs; a trading speed matched to that signal\textquotesingle s horizon, learned (as in an aim-portfolio formulation \cite{r46}) rather than fixed; and risk information it can act on before losses arrive, for instance forecast volatility in the state and a critic that estimates the CVaR of the return-to-go \cite{r20,r21,r54} rather than a penalty on realised history. It would also need regularisation against fitting the training window, since performance fell with training in every setting we examined.

\section{Limitations}\label{s10}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Not an untouched hold-out.} The 2012--2026 test period informed design choices in versions 1--5. The analysis plan pre-specifies the tests of the rerun, not the research design, which was developed while the same period was being examined. The corrected results are therefore a retrospective reassessment, not an independent confirmation; that needs data used in no version, such as a second universe with a new test period or a forward test of frozen models.
\item
  \textbf{Partially observed reward.} The CVaR penalty depends on return history that the state does not contain (\S{}3.4). The negative results for the penalties are results for this formulation: with the rolling CVaR and VaR, the return window or a recurrent risk summary in the state, a penalised agent might learn a different response to tail risk.
\item
  \textbf{Only the cash channel of de-risking is measured.} Risk reduction through the asset mix (duration, credit, concentration, beta) conditional on market volatility is not analysed (\S{}6.5).
\item
  \textbf{Power.} With 5 seeds and 3,527 days, the primary tests can detect Sharpe differences of about 0.21--0.34 with 80\% power. Non-significant results are absence of evidence, not evidence of no effect: none of the non-significant primary comparisons meets the $\pm$0.15 equivalence criterion, so smaller effects of the penalties, or a small advantage or disadvantage of SE against 1/N, cannot be ruled out. More seeds (10--20) would narrow the seed component of the intervals.
\item
  \textbf{Risk comparisons are secondary.} The comparisons of CVaR, drawdown and turnover, central to a study of risk penalties, are uncorrected and exploratory. A pre-specified family of risk hypotheses with multiplicity correction would make them confirmatory.
\item
  \textbf{The factorial design is not fully used.} The primary family tests two simple effects of the 2$\times$2 design; the main effects and the interaction of penalties and smoothing were not tested (\S{}6.2).
\item
  \textbf{Zero cash rate.} Cash earns nothing, and Sharpe ratios use a zero risk-free rate. A Treasury-bill yield would add about each strategy\textquotesingle s average cash weight times the yield to its return, which favours the agents that hold cash over fully invested 1/N: if the yield averaged about 2\% a year, SE (15\% cash) would gain about 0.3 percentage points a year relative to 1/N. Sharpe ratios in excess of the bill rate would be lower for every strategy. We have not rerun the comparisons with a historical bill-rate series.
\item
  \textbf{One market-data universe.} Ten U.S.-listed ETFs at daily frequency, chosen in 2026 from funds that still exist (survivorship and selection). The conclusions concern PPO with this architecture, a \$10m portfolio and this universe; they may differ for single stocks, futures, international markets, intraday data, larger portfolios (where impact matters more), other periods or other RL algorithms.
\item
  \textbf{Costs and liquidity.} Cost parameters are plausible for institutional ETF trading, not calibrated to a desk. ADV is computed from back-adjusted prices, which understates early-sample dollar volume and so overstates early impact costs; this is conservative and mostly penalises high-turnover strategies. Fixed-share orders can leave small negative cash after gaps, financed at 0\%.
\item
  \textbf{Design choices not tuned.} PPO hyper-parameters, training budgets, the single penalty setting (\S{}3.4) and the smoothing speed (\S{}3.2) are fixed, not tuned per variant; warm-started chains accumulate training across folds (\S{}7.2 checks a subset). Each test fold starts in cash, so smoothed agents spend about a month per fold ramping up (\S{}7.1 tests an inherited book).
\item
  \textbf{Simulated markets.} Two seeds per variant and four folds per market; the generator is ours, and its latent alpha may be easier or harder to learn than real signals. They support the ETF results but do not confirm them independently.
\item
  \textbf{No distributional or risk-sensitive RL baseline.} We test reward penalties, not methods that optimise a risk measure of the return-to-go \cite{r21,r54}.
\item
  \textbf{No independent reproduction.} The code, configurations, dependency lock file and output hashes are released so that the results can be reproduced, but no one outside this study has yet done so.
\item
  \textbf{Stress labels} are named historical episodes dated with hindsight; they are used only for analysis.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{s11}

We evaluated tail-risk reward penalties for PPO portfolio allocators under tradable next-open execution, realistic frictions, walk-forward retraining, five training seeds and pre-specified tests, on a test period that earlier versions of this study had already used. In the pre-specified tests, the penalties do not detectably change the Sharpe ratio, slowing execution is the only design change with a significant effect, and the best agent is then neither better than nor shown to be equivalent to equal weighting. A pre-cost timing edge reported in earlier versions was an artifact of filling at a close the agent had already seen, and is withdrawn. Exploratory comparisons suggest that the penalties lower tail risk only as much as holding the same cash in 1/N would, and that no agent raises its cash weight as risk rises. These conclusions concern per-step penalties on realised history that the agent only partly observes; risk-sensitive objectives, risk-aware states, de-risking through the asset mix and genuinely unseen data remain open. For DRL allocators, the fill convention and the trading process deserve at least as much scrutiny as the reward, and the untrained network\textquotesingle s own prior is a benchmark worth reporting.
